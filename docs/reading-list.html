Research profile and selection rules

Prioritize principled Bayesian methodology and statistical computation for complex data: variational inference, expectation propagation, MCMC, scalability, convergence and approximate inference. Favor latent-structure models, generalized linear mixed models, dynamic and state-space models, and network models, especially contributions combining theory and computation. Nonparametric Bayes, survival analysis and spatial statistics are secondary interests when connected to these themes. Give lower priority to deep learning, reinforcement learning or graph neural networks without substantial statistical methodology, routine applications, and software-only descriptions without a methodological contribution.

Laplace Bayesian transport maps for generative modeling of non-Gaussian climate fields

Jacob A. Johnson, Felix Jimenez, Matthias Katzfuss

Bayesian computation

Relevance 3.0/10. Matched topics: Bayesian computation; keyword matches: 2; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Transport maps provide a powerful framework for the generative modeling of complex, non-Gaussian spatial fields, such as those encountered in climate science. By mapping a complicated target distribution to a standard Gaussian reference distribution, they can serve as efficient stochastic surrogates. However, current Bayesian transport maps, which are modeled using autoregressive Gaussian processes, typically rely on empirical Bayes estimates that ignore hyperparameter uncertainty. Here, we contrast this point-estimate approach with a fully Bayesian model that provides posterior uncertainty quantification in transport map hyperparameters. To avoid the high cost of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), we also propose a Laplace approximation to the hyperparameter posteriors, which can be computed at negligible additional computational cost relative to empirical Bayes. We provide numerical evidence and a real-data climate application demonstrating that these Laplace approximations can accurately capture most posterior summaries at a fraction of the cost of MCMC, while maintaining good predictive properties. Thus, our approach offers a scalable, uncertainty-aware framework for the generative modeling of non-Gaussian climate fields.

Inferring Neural Activity Changes under Recording Uncertainty with Transformers and Variational Inference

Nilson Chapagain, Pooja Chapagain

Bayesian computation

Relevance 3.0/10. Matched topics: Bayesian computation; keyword matches: 2; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

A difference in recorded firing rate across days may reflect a change in neuronal activity, missed spikes or recording a different neuron. We present a statistical approach that combines neuron tracking with Bayesian estimates of stationary mean recorded firing rates. Bounds under assumed differences in spike detection connect recorded rates to biological rate changes. We assess recording loss and uncertainty in rate changes in simulations, and count-model fit in recordings. We also compare two transformer estimators with Gaussian and flow variational inference under a shared temporal negative-binomial model. Flow variational inference agrees most closely with reference posteriors, while predictive performance varies across mice. Rate-change intervals contain true simulated changes less often than intended. In UnitMatch recordings from five mice, some inferred changes remain supported when spike detection is allowed to differ between days; others depend on equal detection. These interpretations require a correct neuron match. The approach identifies which inferred rate changes remain supported under specified recording assumptions and where uncertainty limits interpretation.

Bayesian Computation of Copula-based Multivariate Models with an Application to Doping Detection

Emanuele Masillo, Eva Pigi Viltanioti, Nina Deliu, Brunero Liseo

Bayesian computation

Relevance 3.0/10. Matched topics: Bayesian computation; keyword matches: 2; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

The Athlete Biological Passport (ABP) monitors athletes for doping by comparing their biomarker readings against personalized reference ranges. These are estimated via the Bayesian ADAPTIVE model, which analyzes biomarkers separately and ignores their dependence. Building on a recently proposed copula-based multivariate extension of ADAPTIVE, this paper focuses on its computational aspects, introducing a fully joint MCMC algorithm and comparing it with a two-step Bayesian IFM estimator. Simulations show the two procedures perform comparably under a correctly specified copula, but exhibit a genuine trade-off under misspecification: the Bayesian IFM protects marginal inference, while the joint MCMC yields better-calibrated dependence estimates and is markedly faster. The two approaches are tested on a dataset containing ABP profiles of elite athletes, provided by NADO Italia, and collected as part of the project {\it Enhancing the methodological framework for doping detection: from univariate to multivariate testing analysis on Athlete Biological Passport profiles}. Both approaches achieve consistent classification performance, providing anti-doping organizations with two concrete inferential routes for adopting a multivariate ADAPTIVE model, along with guidance on the trade-offs between them.

Scalable Non-Parametric Bayesian Inference of Free Energy Landscapes and Emission Functions from Observations

Eliane Briand, Helmut Grubm\"uller

Dynamic models

Relevance 2.5/10. Matched topics: Dynamic models; keyword matches: 1; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Free-energy landscapes govern processes from protein conformational change to climate oscillations, but are typically observed only indirectly, through observables such as FRET intensity or optical-tweezer transmittance. Neither the landscape shape, nor the emission function mapping latent state to signal, is usually known, and distinct states may produce the same signal. We therefore infer landscape and emission function jointly and non-parametrically, using the exact non-linear Bayesian filtering likelihood, to fully use the information contained in observable trajectories that are often noisy, scarce and costly to acquire. A novel parallel-in-time algorithm makes this tractable: it is up to 27 times faster than sequential filtering on one GPU, scales near-ideally across GPUs, and, given enough resources, has runtime independent of trajectory length. We demonstrate the method on a single-protein optical tweezer recording, where distinct conformations produce the same signal, and beyond biology on the El Nino-Southern Oscillation and financial market volatility.

WAIVES: Weak-form Asymptotically-unbiased Instrumental-Variable Estimation for SDEs

Hamza Ruzayqat, David M. Bortz, Vanja Duki\'c

Dynamic models

Relevance 2.0/10. Matched topics: Dynamic models; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Weak-form methods have transformed inference for deterministic dynamical systems. By integrating the governing equations against smooth, compactly supported test functions, they trade noise-amplifying differentiation for noise-attenuating integration, yielding fast and robust estimates. Extending this idea to stochastic differential equations (SDEs) has proven harder, and a consistent single-trajectory formulation has remained out of reach. Applied naively in the time domain, the weak form makes the regressors and the errors functionals of the same noise, creating endogeneity that leaves weak-form least squares biased and inconsistent. We resolve it with instrumental variables, a classical idea from econometrics. Weak-form integrals over nearby disjoint time windows prove to be valid instruments, correlated with the regressors yet uncorrelated with the error, restoring consistency and asymptotic normality. The resulting generalized-method-of-moments estimator, WAIVES (Weak-form Asymptotically-unbiased Instrumental-Variable Estimation for SDEs), requires only a linear solve, with no likelihood evaluations, filtering, or simulation. It is therefore an order of magnitude faster than exact-likelihood methods where those exist, and it applies unchanged where they do not, with standard errors and calibrated confidence intervals supplied by the moment conditions. It also separates measurement noise from the intrinsic randomness of the dynamics. Across nine example systems, spanning linear, bistable, chaotic, excitable, state-dependent-diffusion, non-polynomial, hypoelliptic, and high-dimensional regimes (up to 120 dimensions), WAIVES recovers nonlinear drift and diffusion with negligible bias and near-nominal interval coverage. We close with two real-data illustrations: a 167-year North Atlantic sea-surface-temperature record and an optical-tweezer calibration.

Global sensitivity analysis of transient regimes in non-Newtonian dam-break flows applied in rheometry

Yuri Taglieri S\'ao, Jo\~ao Batista Pereira, Geraldo de Freitas Maciel

Dynamic models

Relevance 2.0/10. Matched topics: Dynamic models; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Non-Newtonian dam-break flows are a classical problem with numerous engineering applications. These flows are particularly important in alternative rheometry, where they are used to rapidly and affordably estimate rheological properties, such as in the slump test and Bostwick consistometer test. However, the lifting dynamics of the containing obstacle introduce inertial effects that can compromise the accurate estimation of these properties. These effects are influenced by the lifting velocity, the fluid's viscosity, and the initial geometry of the sample. This study aims to quantify the influence of lifting velocity and to provide physical insights into how inertial effects influence the flow. A global sensitivity analysis (GSA) framework adapted for functional outputs (e.g., time series) is employed, using data generated from high-fidelity simulations with ANSYS Fluent. The GSA produces sensitivity curves that track the evolving importance of each input parameter over time. Three distinct flow regimes are identified, namely mobilization, transition and viscous regimes, each governed by different dominant factors. The first two regimes are significantly affected by the lifting velocity, revealing the influence of inertial effects, whereas the viscous regime is dominated by fluid rheology and shows a increasing importance of the yield stress. These results are contextualized in terms of traditional rheometry tests.

A Generative Model for Higher-Order Network Dynamics

Valentina S\'anchez, Theodore Papamarkou, Gonzalo N\'apoles, \c{C}i\c{c}ek G\"uven, Koen V. Haak

Dynamic models

Relevance 2.0/10. Matched topics: Dynamic models; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Conventional network representations inferred from multivariate time series describe statistical relationships between pairs of components, even when the underlying dynamics involve group interactions that cannot be represented by pairwise interactions alone. Several multivariate information-theoretic measures have been proposed to characterise dependence beyond pairs. However, due to a lack of systems with known generating mechanisms, these measures have not been carefully validated or compared for empirical use. To address this, we here introduce a generative model of stochastic network dynamics with pairwise and higher-order interactions specified by the user. The higher-order interactions are represented by directed hyperedges, and the model provides a controlled benchmark for evaluating information-theoretic measures. Removing only the higher-order term gives a pairwise-only reference. The experiments test whether a measure detects changes induced by the higher-order term relative to this reference and recovers the participating nodes and their source-target roles. A separate experiment tests discrimination between a product coupling and a specified nonlinear pairwise control, with equal root-mean-square coupling magnitude under the pairwise-only reference. Several measures detected sufficiently strong higher-order interactions across the tested network topologies and coupling functions. In the separate discrimination experiment, the measures also separated the product coupling from the specified nonlinear pairwise control. Recovery, however, became harder as the number of candidate interactions grew. The framework therefore provides a testbed where the link between higher-order signatures and mechanisms can be tested, rather than assumed, before measures are applied to empirical data.

Improving External Prediction in New Clusters with Mixed-Effects Models: Implications for Multicenter Studies

Jiawa Zhang, Hyungrok Do, Myeonggyun Lee

Latent and mixed models

Relevance 2.0/10. Matched topics: Latent and mixed models; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Prediction models developed from multicenter data are often applied to new centers. A linear mixed model (LMM) captures between-center heterogeneity through random effects. However, a new center's random effect is unknown, so the LMM predicts from fixed effects alone, as does a standard linear model (LM) that ignores clustering. The two models thus differ only in how fixed effects are estimated, and it is unclear whether the LMM predicts better. We decompose the prediction error in a new center into fixed-effect estimation error, between-center variation, and residual variation. Because the last two components are identical for both models, any LMM advantage must come from more precise fixed-effect estimates. In simulations across diverse designs and random-effect structures, the LMM generally achieved lower mean squared error than the LM. Relative gains increased with the number of predictors, between-center heterogeneity, and imbalance in center sizes, and decreased as training data grew. Modeling a random slope yielded additional gains that increased with between-center variation in the predictor effect. In a stress test using held-out eICU hospitals selected for strong outcome heterogeneity, a random-intercept LMM predicted ICU length of stay more accurately than an LM. These findings clarify the conditions under which mixed models can improve prediction in new centers and can inform model development for multicenter studies.

Fast Bayesian Methods for Uncertainty Quantification in Sparse AI Evaluation

Dario Deniz Ergun, Haohua Wang, Brendon Tao, Jonah Kollenberg, Chen Ye Gan

Relevance 0.5/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 1; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Estimating how an AI system will perform on a new benchmark need not require another large model. We use public evaluation counts to choose a weak Beta prior, then update it exactly from at most 31 revealed outcomes. The predictor uses no item semantics, pretrained weights, or inference API. Its single online evaluation in the AIMS competition returned a team-reported Brier ALC of 0.188477, a 24.6% reduction from the constant-0.5 reference. The complete submission is 7,754 bytes; a server microbenchmark measures 12.71 microseconds per prediction with 31 matching labels. We describe how source evaluations are compressed into 301 prior means and show why a concentration of two lets new evidence rapidly determine the prediction. The resulting posterior gives exact uncertainty about the modelled success rate without numerical integration or neural inference. A public evaluation covers 79 system-benchmark pairs and eight seeds, reaching Brier ALC 0.200250 for the submitted method. Accompanying code and sufficient statistics reconstruct the fitted prior byte for byte and replay all 632 evaluation trajectories. The result demonstrates how much predictive value a compact Bayesian model can extract from sparse target feedback at very low online cost.

Latent space dynamic network actor models with position-rate link

Alberto Caimo, Isabella Gollini

Relevance 0.5/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 1; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Relational event data record time-stamped interactions between actors. Dynamic network actor models represent each interaction as two separate decisions: when an actor becomes active and whom they select. Modelling these decisions separately cannot establish whether the most active actors are also the most frequently selected. We propose a latent space dynamic network actor model in which sender and receiver positions capture receiver choice, while a position-rate link relates latent positions to activity levels. The link uses two summaries invariant to latent position updates: latent popularity, measuring how much an actor is favoured by others, and excess self-affinity, capturing preference for similar actors beyond that explained by popularity. We adopt a Bayesian modular approach, estimating latent positions from receiver choices alone. Using e-mail data from a manufacturing company, we find that the most active employees are also the most frequently selected recipients. With multiple latent dimensions, preference for similar colleagues contributes less. Latent positions improve receiver-choice prediction, while the position-rate link captures differences in actor activity.

LIC-Audit: How Comparable Are Reported Gains in Learned Image Compression?

Haisheng Fu, Jie Liang, Runyu Yang, Feng Ding, Xin Dong, Zixiao Jin, Jingning Han

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Learned image compression (LIC) papers often report BD-rate improvements of only a few percent. They are measured against VTM reference software whose version keeps changing: the claims we audit name 7 versions, 5 in 2025 papers alone. But how comparable are these numbers across papers, and what evidence should future papers give for such small gains? We introduce LIC-Audit, an analysis of 353 released rate-distortion curves from 36 repositories, and a controlled re-evaluation of eight public codec releases with 28,193 encode-decode records. We identify three problems. First, different VTM reference runs change BD-rates by up to 3.56 percentage points, and on CLIC Pro published numbers order 10 of 55 method pairs differently from a common computation; comparing codecs directly over a fixed quality range removes this dependence. Second, releases use different and mostly undocumented conventions for counting bits, computing PSNR, and padding images. Under consistent measurement, the GLIC method's 1.00% lead over the HPCM method on Tecnick becomes a 1.43% deficit, changing only the padding reverses the comparison of HPCM and CMIC, and most gaps narrow when MS-SSIM replaces PSNR. Third, average curves hide how much results vary from image to image, so whether a small gain is statistically significant depends on the assumed variation. An optimistic assumption calls 12.0% of comparisons between equally good codecs significant on test sets as small as Kodak, instead of 5%; a cautious one leaves 18 of 45 differences between recent codecs undecided, yet per-image results show 12 of them to be significant. Beyond auditing past results, LIC-Audit provides a versioned evaluation protocol, a public leaderboard website with a scoring tool, source-linked benchmark data, per-image measurements, and reporting guidelines, to help future LIC papers make comparable and verifiable claims of progress.

Bits, Not Boxes: Label-Free Layer Selection for Visual Localization in VLMs

Haisheng Fu, Gexin Huang, Haikai Zhao, Jie Liang, Zhenman Fang, Jingning Han, Xiaoxiao Li

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Attention maps cheaply show where a vision-language model (VLM) looks, but attention sinks make them unreliable for localization. Contrasting the question with a generic prompt suppresses sinks, yet only some layers localize the queried object. Existing methods find these layers with box annotations or fixed rules. We show that attention itself can identify them. A question changes how much a head attends to the image and where this attention goes. The two effects separate exactly, and the second, measured in bits on unlabeled questions, indicates which layers localize. WhereBits turns these where-to-look bits into a layer prior and combines it with per-head contrastive scores. Its three design constants were fixed once on labeled COCO data, and a new model or resolution needs only unlabeled questions. Across eight VLMs, WhereBits matches the pointing accuracy of box-tuned layers on COCO and RefCOCO and ranks target tokens better. This advantage holds on a held-out model family and at higher resolutions. Its COCO pointing accuracy is 0.72, against 0.22 for mean attention. In training-free zoom-in, its crops raise V* accuracy by 6.0 points under forced-choice evaluation.

Wasserstein Target Diagram for Model Evaluation

Tiantian Yang, Dongwei Chen

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

The target diagram is often used in geosciences, which provides a visualization tool for model evaluation and shows the temporal or spatial matching of data. However, its construction requires paired data of equal sample sizes, limiting its application to model comparisons involving unpaired samples or probability distributions. In this study, we propose the Wasserstein target diagram based on the 2-Wasserstein distance from optimal transport. Unlike the original target diagram, the Wasserstein target diagram does not require paired data of equal sizes and evaluates models from the probability distribution perspective. We further establish that the two target diagrams coincide when the given paired data are comonotone, which is common in climate science, such as cumulative precipitation and solar radiation. Through two simulation studies and an application to wind speed distribution fitting, we demonstrate that the proposed diagram can detect the probability distribution discrepancies that the original target diagram may not distinguish. The Wasserstein target diagram therefore lifts the model evaluation to the probability level and provides a complementary approach to estimating the agreement in probability distributions.

Balancing Partition Forest for Causal Weighting

Felipe Angelim, Alessandro Leite

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Balancing weights are used in observational causal inference to construct a population in which the treatment is independent of observed pretreatment covariates. The ideal weight is the ratio between the product of the covariate and treatment marginals and their joint distribution, for binary and continuous treatments alike. We propose Balancing Partition Tree (BPT) and Balancing Partition Forest (BPF), which estimate this ratio on a learned rectangular partition of covariate--treatment space. Each split maximizes the gain in a Bregman divergence computed from joint and marginal counts. Two choices of divergence yield criteria that maximize the mutual information captured by the partition or reduce the squared error of the weights. We show that the population residual of either criterion bounds the bias of inverse-probability-weighted and doubly robust response estimators that use the population partition weights. Across 17 synthetic and semi-synthetic settings and three response estimators, BPF obtains the lowest mean RMSE rank among seven weight estimators, and a single tree fits in 0.14 seconds on 16,512 observations.

Federated Differential Privacy with Batch Contamination

Jason K. C. Ng, Thomas B. Berrett, Yi Yu

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

We study mean estimation under federated differential privacy (FDP) constraints and batch contamination, considering both Gaussian and heavy-tailed distributions. In the homogeneous setting, where sample sizes and privacy budgets are constant across servers, we show that the costs of privacy and contamination disentangle. Under a bounded $v$th moment condition, with $v \in (1, \infty)$, the cost of batch contamination is of order $(\varepsilon/n)^{2-2/v}\vee \varepsilon^2/n$, where $\varepsilon$ is the contamination level and $n$ is the per-batch sample size. This reveals a phase transition at $\varepsilon\asymp n^{1-v/2}$, for $v > 2$, which is the first of its kind in the literature. We develop a two-stage procedure that achieves the minimax rate up to poly-logarithmic factors. A key component is a private smoothed voting procedure tailored to the FDP setting that provides optimal sensitivity control. For heterogeneous batch sample sizes and privacy budgets, we introduce a batch partitioning framework that groups batches with comparable per-batch sample sizes and privacy budgets. Surprisingly, the minimax rate is determined, up to poly-logarithmic factors, by the most informative group of batches. Consequently, in some regimes, it is minimax optimal to use only an arbitrarily small proportion of the data set. Heterogeneity also introduces interactions between the costs of privacy and contamination, in contrast to their disentanglement in the homogeneous setting. For unknown contamination level, we propose an adaptive, near-minimax optimal Lepski-type extension for the heterogeneous Gaussian setting, designed specifically to prevent privacy leakage by ensuring no repeated use of data points.

Spatial Extremes on Domains with Physical Barriers

Chien-Chung Wang, Benjamin A. Shaby

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

We propose a model for spatial extremes what realistically accommodates nonconvex domains. Most existing models assume that tail dependence between locations depends only on their separation. Even if extended to be anisotropic or nonstationary, they still cannot accommodate features between points that may mediate the dependence. This assumption limits their applicability in settings where physical barriers like mountains, coastlines, or islands may attenuate or even block dependence in extreme events. In this work, we use the idea of a visibility graph to modify an existing spatial random scale model. This results in a tail dependence model that naturally accommodates nonconvex spatial domains, where the nonconvex features are interpreted as physical barriers. Furthermore, the model inherits key properties from the scale mixture like the ability to represent asymptotic independence at long ranges and either asymptotic dependence or asymptotic independence at short ranges. We apply our model to two applications in which physical barriers feature prominently in determining the spatial structure of extreme events. The first is precipitation in the South Island of New Zealand, where the Southern Alps shelter the eastern region from the effects of atmospheric rivers which arrive from the west. The second is significant wave heights in the North Atlantic, where the Faroe Islands tend to block the effects of extratropical cyclones traveling into the North Sea.

Instrument indeterminacy: separating uncertainty in POVM effects and its neutrosophic reading

Fernando A. Otero

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Neutrosophic annotation frameworks for quantum measurement attach an external triple $(t,i,f)$ to each Born probability, and explicitly leave open how indeterminacy could be defined operationally. We show that indeterminacy cannot be recovered from outcome probabilities, but can be computed from the pair (state, effect). For a binary POVM $\{A, I-A\}$ we define the instrument indeterminacy $\mathcal{I}_\rho(A) = 4 \mathrm{Tr}(\rho(A-A^2))$, which vanishes exactly when the state only sees the sharp part of the effect. The normalized Gini index of the outcome splits as $4TF = \mathcal{I}_\rho(A) + 4 \mathrm{Var}_\rho(A)$, a law of total variance that separates the instrument contribution from the state contribution; the identity is a special case of Massar's POVM uncertainty operator, and our contribution is its neutrosophic reading. Moving the system-apparatus cut to a Naimark dilation transfers exactly $\mathcal{I}_\rho(A)$ from indeterminacy to variance, which yields the missing operational definition. We extend the decomposition to $m$-outcome POVMs, relate the minimum indeterminacy of two incompatible unbiased qubit observables to their commutator through Busch's joint-measurability criterion, and show that $\mathcal{I}_\rho(A)$ equals twice the disagreement probability of a repeated L\"uders measurement. The resulting estimator needs neither the state nor the effect and, in simulation, requires 2.5 to 6 times fewer shots than detector tomography for the same error.

Confounding Heterogeneity in Multivariate Sensitivity Analysis for Observational Studies

Anthony Frazier, Siyu Heng, Wen Zhou

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

In observational studies, unmeasured confounding may bias causal conclusions even after matching on measured confounders. Sensitivity analysis is therefore usually needed to assess the robustness of causal conclusions to unmeasured confounding. We consider multivariate sensitivity analysis that assesses the causal effect of a single treatment on multiple outcomes. We identify an inherent feature of such analyses, which we call ``confounding heterogeneity'': different outcomes may be affected by distinct unmeasured confounders, and a common unmeasured confounder may affect different outcomes to varying degrees. Building on matched observational studies, we formulate sensitivity analysis frameworks that accommodate confounding heterogeneity. We show that Type I error control for many existing sensitivity analysis methods is guaranteed under sensitivity analysis models and modes of inference that differ fundamentally from those used in the classic framework. We study asymptotic power using design sensitivity and Bahadur-Rosenbaum efficiency and examine finite-sample power through numerical studies. Our results show that the structure of unmeasured confounding affects sensitivity analysis, and that widely used single-parameter frameworks may mischaracterize the sensitivity of a causal conclusion to unmeasured confounding when confounding heterogeneity is considered. Simulations and a data application illustrate the practical importance of confounding heterogeneity in multivariate sensitivity analyses.

When Do LLMs Model the Right Target? Problem Formulation in High-Stakes Risk Prediction

Jennifer Zhang, Amanda Coston

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

Generative AI is increasingly used to assist with data science tasks, yet fundamental questions remain about its ability to correctly formulate analytical problems. We investigate how well large language models (LLMs) can formulate prediction problems when developing models for high-stakes decision making. We focus on one particular challenge to validity: decision dependency, which arises when historical decisions affect observed outcomes. We evaluate this aspect of problem formulation in LLM-generated code and explanations across child-welfare, healthcare, and employment settings, using 11 models from OpenAI and Anthropic and seven prompt conditions. We assess whether the LLM-generated code implements a procedure appropriate for estimating the intended outcome under the study's assumptions and whether the explanation acknowledges decision dependency. We find that LLMs do not reliably address decision dependency without guidance in the prompt. When prompts specify the target, the assumption needed to estimate it, and the estimation procedure, target-aligned implementation reaches 99-100%. Among frontier models, a simple prompt description of how decisions affect outcomes appears sufficient to elicit target-aligned implementation. Some responses also acknowledge decision dependency without addressing it in their code. Our findings suggest that analysts should not rely on LLMs to correctly formulate prediction problems without explicit methodological guidance and careful review. We propose validity-centered guidelines for LLM-assisted predictive modeling, covering prompt formulation, validation of the target and estimation procedure, and identification of key assumptions. Establishing validity is a necessary early step in evaluating whether to deploy a predictive model in a high-stakes setting, but deployment requires weighing broader ethical, institutional, and practical considerations.

A Free Lunch: Gradient Averaging Achieves Optimal Statistical Efficiency in Newton Sketch

Haoxuan Wang, Jianliang Ye, Sen Na

Relevance 0/10. Matched topics: none; keyword matches: 0; preferred authors: 0.

Abstract and links

We study statistical inference for online sketched Newton methods, in which each Newton system is approximately solved by an inner randomized sketching solver. Existing work reveals a computational--statistical trade-off: the sketching solver (and its accelerated variants) reduces the per-step cost of Newton methods from $O(d^3)$ to $O(d^2)$, but the randomness introduced by sketching also inflates the limiting covariance of both the last and the averaged Newton iterates. In this work, we address this trade-off via gradient averaging. We show that simply replacing the stochastic gradient with a moving average at each step can mitigate, and in some cases eliminate, the trade-off in Newton sketch. Importantly, gradient averaging requires no additional oracle evaluations and leaves the contraction bound of the inner sketching solver unchanged. In particular, we prove that the last sketched Newton iterate with gradient averaging attains a limiting covariance no larger than that without gradient averaging, while the averaged iterate even attains the minimax optimal limiting covariance, matching that of the exact Newton method. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis showing that randomness introduced to improve computational efficiency need not compromise statistical efficiency as long as proper gradient estimates are used. The key analytical challenge is that gradient averaging destroys the crucial martingale difference structure underlying central limit theorem arguments. We overcome this challenge by analyzing the coupled two-timescale recursions governing the iterate and the gradient estimator. Numerical experiments on linear and logistic regression support our theoretical covariance comparisons.
